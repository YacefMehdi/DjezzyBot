\textbf{Abstract}\\[0.3cm]
This thesis presents the design and implementation of \textbf{DjezzyBot}, a multilingual virtual assistant for subscribers of the Algerian operator Djezzy. Call centers handle a large volume of repetitive, factual questions, and there is no NLP tool fitted to the mix of languages used in Algeria (French, Modern Standard Arabic, English, and Algerian Darija). DjezzyBot answers questions about offers and prices \emph{only} from text scraped from Djezzy's own websites, through a \textbf{RAG} (\textit{Retrieval-Augmented Generation}) pipeline built entirely on free, open-source models that run on a single Google Colab T4 GPU (15~GB). Retrieval is a rule-based intent router with \numRoutes{} routes placed in front of a dense \textbf{FAISS} index (\texttt{multilingual-e5} embeddings, exact cosine search); generation uses \textbf{Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct} quantized to 4-bit. The assistant also accepts and produces speech (Whisper for recognition, Coqui XTTS-v2 for synthesis). Out-of-domain questions are handled by two layers --- a low similarity floor and the model's own domain-scope rule --- a design we justify with a calibration experiment on about 95 queries, which shows that the embedding score alone cannot separate real questions from off-topic ones. On \numTestsTotal{} acceptance scenarios across four languages, the system passes \numTestsPass{}/\numTestsTotal{} (about 92\,\%).

\textbf{Keywords:} LLM, RAG, Chatbot, NLP, Darija, Qwen~2.5, FAISS, multilingual-e5, Whisper, XTTS, Virtual Assistant, Djezzy.

\section*{Context of the Study}
\humantext{Algeria's telecom market is going through a major digital shift, driven by widespread mobile internet access and offer catalogues that keep growing.} \sourced{According to the Regulatory Authority for Post and Electronic Communications (ARPCE), the number of mobile subscribers in Algeria reached about 54~million in the fourth quarter of 2024 \cite{arpce2024}, a penetration rate of roughly 117\,\% for a population of 46.7~million on 1~January 2024 (projected at 47.4~million in 2025) \cite{ons2025}.} \sourced{Three operators share this market: Mobilis (a subsidiary of Algérie Télécom), Ooredoo (Qatari group), and Djezzy (part of the VEON group), the last of which counts more than 14~million active subscribers.}

\sourced{Operators like Djezzy regularly launch new plans --- prepaid offers (Legend, iZZY), postpaid offers (Legend Max), student offers (Student Campuce), value-added services (E-Flexy, DjezzyNet), and international roaming bundles --- to meet the expectations of an increasingly connected and demanding customer base.} \humantext{In this competitive setting, giving customers a smooth experience and helping them find the right information instantly have become real levers for keeping them loyal.}

\humantext{At the same time, AI is moving fast thanks to Large Language Models (LLMs).} \humantext{Models such as GPT-4, Llama 3, and Qwen show every day how well they can capture the nuances of language and write text that reads as human.} \sourced{To address the factual weaknesses of these models, the RAG paradigm (\textit{Retrieval-Augmented Generation}) introduced by Lewis et al. \cite{lewis2020rag} pairs the parametric memory of a pre-trained generator with an external, non-parametric memory.} \sourced{This grounds a model's answers in a factual knowledge base, which opens the door to virtual assistants that give precise, contextual, and verifiable answers.}

\section*{Problem Statement}
\humantext{Even though Djezzy's official website and mobile app hold a lot of information, customers often run into a fragmentation problem.} \humantext{Getting a clear answer about a promotion, the rules for activating a SIM, or the exact price of a roaming option stays tedious: the user usually has to dig through dense, sometimes maze-like web menus.}

\humantext{Stuck, customers end up calling phone support in large numbers. This crowds the lines, lengthens the wait, and wears down their patience.} \sourced{Industry studies report that more than 60\,\% of calls to telecom support centers are basic information questions (prices, plans, activation) that could be automated \cite{gartner2023}.}

\humantext{Handing this support to classic chatbots (rigid keyword matching or decision trees) quickly falls short, for several reasons:}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \humantext{\textbf{The language barrier:} Algerians commonly \textit{code-switch}, mixing Arabic, French, and Darija, often typed in ``Arabizi'' (for example ``3tini l'offre''). Older bots get completely lost.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Lack of flexibility:} A pre-scripted system cannot summarize or compare two plans the way a human would.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Vendor lock-in:} Relying on paid APIs such as OpenAI raises heavy concerns about cost, technical independence, and leaking private data.}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Proposed Solution: DjezzyBot}
\humantext{To meet these challenges, this end-of-studies project in Intelligent Computer Systems Engineering (ISII) proposes the design and implementation of \textbf{DjezzyBot}.} \humantext{The idea is a virtual companion available day and night, which you can talk to by keyboard or by microphone.}

\humantext{Unlike solutions built on closed commercial APIs, this project makes one strong technical bet: it uses \textit{open-source} technology only.} \humantext{DjezzyBot is built on a Large Language Model (specifically Qwen2.5 from Alibaba Cloud) coupled with the RAG (\textit{Retrieval-Augmented Generation}) architecture.} \humantext{This way, the machine first pulls the exact information from a FAISS database before writing its answer. We remove the risk of inventing facts (hallucinations) while keeping full control of the data in-house.}

\humantext{For languages, the system takes a deliberately simple route: rather than translating questions, it embeds French, Arabic, English, and Darija \emph{directly} with a multilingual embedding model, and lets Qwen2.5 reply in the user's language. On top of dense vector search, a rule-based intent router reads each question first and detects brand names (e.g. ``Campuce'', ``iZZY'') by exact lexical matching, so an offer is found even when the embedding model does not recognize an invented commercial name.}

\section*{Project Objectives}
\humantext{The overall goal of this thesis is to build an end-to-end solution. It breaks down into several technical sub-objectives:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Building the knowledge base:} automated extraction (\textit{web scraping} with Playwright and BeautifulSoup) of data from Djezzy's official sites, aggressive HTML cleaning, and storing the information as JSON.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Implementing the RAG pipeline:} embedding the text with a multilingual model (\texttt{multilingual-e5}) orchestrated by LangChain, stored in a FAISS vector index with per-page chunking.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Designing a rule-based intent router:} a search component that reads each question first and routes it to one of \numRoutes{} strategies (competitor, roaming, catalogue, comparison, budget, named-offer, normal) before touching the vector store.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Multilingual support:} handling Arabic, French, English, and Darija \emph{without} a translation layer --- the questions are embedded directly and answered in the user's language, with a lexicon used only for language detection and query expansion.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Voice integration:} speech recognition (\textit{Speech-to-Text} with Whisper) and speech synthesis (\textit{Text-to-Speech} with Coqui XTTS-v2), covering the target languages.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Building the user interface:} an interactive web interface (with Gradio) where text and voice share a single conversation.}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Structure of the Thesis}
\humantext{To follow the whole approach, the thesis is organized into five main parts:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Chapter 1} introduces Djezzy, its role on the national market, and the analysis that led us to start this project.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Chapter 2} reviews the relevant literature, from basic NLP up to Transformers, LLMs, and RAG and voice architectures.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Chapter 3} dives into the architecture of DjezzyBot itself, explaining how the data is acquired and processed and how the modules fit together.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Chapter 4} tells the implementation story: our working environment, the hardware constraints we faced, and the main algorithms.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Chapter 5} puts the bot through a set of acceptance tests, weighs the local stack against hosted APIs, and points to future work.}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Activities and Offer Portfolio}

\humantext{Djezzy's core business is still consumer mobile telephony. To fit a very diverse Algerian society, the brand has split its offer into several distinct families.}

\section{Needs Analysis}

\subsection{Context and Problem Identification}

\humantext{During our development work, we saw that the call centers of an operator like Djezzy handle a very large daily volume of recurring, factual requests: balance lookups, plan details, activating an option, roaming conditions, top-up procedures, and so on.} \humantext{Even when the answers are basic, this constant flow overloads the agents, creates endless queues, and eventually frustrates the subscriber.}

\humantext{A second difficulty, specific to the Algerian context, appeared during fieldwork: subscribers express themselves in French, Modern Standard Arabic, or Algerian dialectal Arabic (Darija), interchangeably. No available NLP solution is, to date, fitted to this multilingual reality in national telecom.}

\humantext{It is from this double observation --- overloaded customer-relations centers and the absence of a language tool fitted to the local context --- that the DjezzyBot project started. The goal is a conversational assistant that can answer subscribers' most frequent questions automatically, in French and Arabic, in writing and by voice, relying only on a knowledge base built from the operator's official data.}

\subsection{Actors}

\humantext{Before defining the technical specifications, it helps to list who will use the bot. We distinguish two profiles:}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{The Djezzy subscriber} is the main actor. They interact with DjezzyBot through a conversational interface (text or voice) to get an answer. They may use French, Arabic, or Darija, and their questions typically concern commercial offers, prices, top-ups, roaming options, or general procedures tied to the operator's services.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{The administrator / technical team} is responsible for updating the knowledge base (the JSON files produced by the scraping module from Djezzy's official site) and for supervising the system. They do not interact with the bot in real time, but they directly determine the quality and freshness of the available information.}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Requirements}

\humantext{From our analysis of the domain and the kinds of questions typically sent to a telecom operator, we identified the following requirements:}

\textbf{Functional requirements:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \humantext{Understand questions phrased in natural language, in French, Modern Standard Arabic, or Darija, without forcing the user into a strict wording.}
    \item \humantext{Retrieve the relevant information from the Djezzy knowledge base and present it in a coherent, natural answer.}
    \item \humantext{Offer a voice interface (recognition and synthesis) alongside the text interface.}
    \item \humantext{Politely and clearly refuse any request unrelated to the operator's services (out-of-domain questions), without blocking the conversation.}
    \item \humantext{Work autonomously from the knowledge base alone, with no connection to real-time subscriber-account APIs.}
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Non-functional requirements:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Factual accuracy:} answers must be strictly grounded in the knowledge base; any hallucinated price or commercial condition would be unacceptable here.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Response time:} under 10~seconds for a text answer; a wider tolerance for a voice answer (STT + inference + TTS included).}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Hardware constraint:} the system must run on an NVIDIA T4 GPU (15~GB of VRAM), representative of the resources available on Google Colab, which puts strict limits on model size and format.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Easy updates:} since prices change quickly, adding an offer must be done instantly by simply updating the knowledge base, with no need to retrain the AI.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Robustness:} the system must handle ambiguous queries, spelling mistakes, and dialectal variation without service interruption.}
\end{itemize}

\chapter{State of the Art and Theoretical Foundations}

\section{Introduction}
\humantext{In this chapter, we give an overview of the key technologies for building a modern conversational assistant. We first walk through the history of NLP up to the Transformer and LLM revolution. We then look in detail at the RAG architecture for factual grounding, the quantization methods that save resources, vector databases, and finally the speech recognition and synthesis tools suited to a dialectal context.}

\section{Evolution of Natural Language Processing (NLP)}

\subsection{From Rule-Based Systems to Machine Learning}
\humantext{Natural Language Processing (NLP) is the area of AI that gives machines the ability to analyze and produce language. In its early days, NLP relied on hand-written rules and static lexicons. These systems worked well in very narrow settings, but maintaining them quickly became unmanageable as the application domain grew.}

\humantext{The arrival of Machine Learning between the 1990s and 2000s favored purely statistical approaches. With models such as HMMs or SVMs, it became possible to train algorithms to classify text automatically or to identify grammatical roles (\textit{POS tagging}).}

\subsection{The Deep Learning Era: RNNs and LSTMs}
\sourced{Deep Learning then transformed the field with Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and their LSTM (\textit{Long Short-Term Memory}) variant introduced by Hochreiter and Schmidhuber \cite{hochreiter1997lstm}.} \humantext{These networks made sequence processing possible thanks to a kind of internal memory of past words. Despite this progress, several major obstacles remained:}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \humantext{A \textbf{strictly sequential} architecture, which processes words one by one and prevents any speed-up through parallelization.}
    \item \humantext{The \textbf{vanishing gradient} problem, which sharply limits the network's ability to retain information over very long sentences.}
    \item \humantext{The inability to use \textbf{future context} (since the meaning of a word is sometimes set by the words that follow it).}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Word Embeddings}
\sourced{A major advance was the introduction of \textit{word embeddings}, notably Word2Vec \cite{mikolov2013word2vec} and GloVe \cite{pennington2014glove}.} \humantext{The core idea is to represent each word as a dense numerical vector. In this new space, words with similar meaning end up close together. The famous expression} $\vec{king} - \vec{man} + \vec{woman} \approx \vec{queen}$ \humantext{shows how these vectors naturally encode semantics.}

\humantext{However, these embeddings are \textbf{static}: the word ``Legend'' always has the same vector, whether it refers to a Djezzy plan or a historical figure.} \humantext{This polysemy weakness is exactly what the Transformer architecture fixed, by introducing dynamic, contextual embeddings.}

\section{The Transformer Architecture and the Attention Mechanism}

\subsection{The Self-Attention Mechanism}
\sourced{In 2017, the landmark paper \textit{``Attention Is All You Need''} by Vaswani et al. \cite{vaswani2017attention} introduced the Transformer architecture, reshaping the foundations of NLP. It replaces recurrence (RNN/LSTM) entirely with the \textbf{self-attention} mechanism, letting the model process all the words in a sentence \textbf{at once} rather than sequentially.}

\humantext{In practice, attention builds three specific matrices for each token:}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Query (Q)}: the search made by the target word against the rest of the sentence.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Key (K)}: an identification key that signals the information the word can provide.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Value (V)}: the raw meaning, or semantic value, of the word itself.}
\end{itemize}

\humantext{The final attention weighting is then given by:}
\begin{equation}
    \text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V
\end{equation}
\sourced{where $d_k$ is the dimension of the key vectors, acting as a normalization factor that stabilizes the gradients \cite{vaswani2017attention}.}

\subsection{The Encoder-Decoder Architecture}
\humantext{The original Transformer is split into two main blocks:}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \humantext{The \textbf{encoder}: analyzes the input sentence and produces context-rich representations.}
    \item \humantext{The \textbf{decoder}: builds the answer one word at a time, using both the encoder's analysis and what it has already written.}
\end{itemize}

\humantext{By stacking many attention layers (often between 6 and 96) equipped with \textit{Multi-Head Attention}, the system can perceive the syntax, semantics, and pragmatics of a sentence at the same time.}

\subsection{From BERT to GPT: Two Paradigms}
\humantext{Two major approaches grew out of this invention:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \sourced{\textbf{BERT} (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers) \cite{devlin2019bert}: encoder-only, it excels at understanding tasks (classification, information extraction, \textit{question answering}). Its bidirectional training lets it consider the left \textit{and} right context of each word.}
    \item \sourced{\textbf{GPT} (Generative Pre-trained Transformer) \cite{brown2020gpt3}: decoder-only, it is optimized for text generation. Each new word is predicted from all the previous words (causal, one-directional attention).}
\end{itemize}

\humantext{It is the GPT paradigm (decoder-only) that gave rise to the modern Large Language Models used in this project.}

\section{Large Language Models (LLMs)}

\subsection{Definition and Principles}
\humantext{Concretely, LLMs are Transformer networks with billions of parameters that have ingested huge libraries of text without direct supervision. Their training usually has two main stages:}
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Pre-training}: the model simply practices guessing the next word in a text, which along the way gives it an excellent command of grammar and a broad base of general knowledge.}
    \item \sourced{\textbf{Fine-tuning / instruction tuning}: the model is specialized to follow human instructions, through reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) \cite{ouyang2022rlhf}.}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Open-Source LLMs}
\humantext{Although commercial tools (such as OpenAI's ChatGPT) made this advance known to the public, many researchers now prefer freely available (\textit{open-source}) models. They avoid steep costs and protect the privacy of the data being processed. Table~\ref{tab:llm_comparison} highlights some major players in this open ecosystem for 2025--2026.}

\end{itemize}

\section{Tokenization}

\subsection{Principles of Tokenization}
\humantext{A neural network does not read letters the way we do: it needs to convert text into a sequence of \textbf{tokens} (word fragments turned into numbers). This step, called tokenization, is fundamental because it sets the size of the model's vocabulary, which heavily affects its speed and efficiency.}

\subsection{Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE)}
\sourced{The BPE (\textit{Byte-Pair Encoding}) algorithm, proposed by Sennrich et al. \cite{sennrich2016bpe}, is the most widely used tokenization method in modern LLMs. It works iteratively:}
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \humantext{Start by listing every character (letter, digit, punctuation mark) present in the training texts.}
    \item \humantext{On each pass, the algorithm finds the two fragments that most often follow each other and merges them into a new token.}
    \item \humantext{This merging repeats until a final dictionary of the desired size is reached (usually between 32{,}000 and 150{,}000 entries).}
\end{enumerate}

\humantext{For example, the word ``forfait'' would first be \texttt{[f, o, r, f, a, i, t]}, then merged into \texttt{[for, fait]} over the iterations.}

\subsection{SentencePiece}
\sourced{SentencePiece \cite{kudo2018sentencepiece} is a popular BPE implementation that processes raw text without space-based pre-tokenization, which makes it well suited to languages with no explicit word separators (such as Arabic or Chinese).} \humantext{Qwen 2.5 uses a SentencePiece variant with a vocabulary of 151{,}643 tokens.}

\section{The RAG Paradigm (Retrieval-Augmented Generation)}

\subsection{Motivations}
\humantext{Despite their impressive power, plain language models run into two deal-breaking problems:}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Stale knowledge:} an LLM's knowledge is frozen at its training date. A model trained in 2024 does not know Djezzy's 2025 plans.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Hallucinations:} LLMs can produce false information phrased convincingly (for example, inventing a ``200~GB for 500~DA'' plan that does not exist).}
\end{itemize}

\sourced{To address this, Lewis et al. \cite{lewis2020rag} proposed the \textbf{RAG} (\textit{Retrieval-Augmented Generation}) architecture, which combines an LLM's generation power with an external information-retrieval system; a broader overview of the field is given by Gao et al. \cite{gao2023ragsurvey}.}

\subsection{How RAG Works}
\humantext{A RAG system is organized around two clearly separate cycles (shown in Figure~\ref{fig:rag_pipeline}):}

\subsubsection{Indexing Phase (offline)}
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Collection:} the source documents (e.g. Djezzy's web pages) are gathered by web scraping.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Chunking:} the documents are split into fixed-size segments (in our system, 1{,}200 characters) with an overlap (120 characters) so that information is not cut between two segments.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Embedding:} each piece of text is then converted into a mathematical vector by a dedicated model (detailed in Section~\ref{sec:embeddings}).}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Storage:} these vectors are finally placed in a special database optimized for mathematical search (see Section~\ref{sec:faiss}).}
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Inference Phase (online)}
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Query embedding:} as soon as the subscriber asks a question, it goes through the same conversion into a vector.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Retrieval:} the algorithm measures the distance between the question's vector and those in the database, instantly bringing back the $k$ semantically closest fragments.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Augmentation:} the retrieved information is literally pasted, invisibly, into the prompt sent to the LLM.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Generation:} the LLM writes an answer based \textbf{only} on the facts in the provided context.}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Embedding Models}
\label{sec:embeddings}

\subsection{From Word Embeddings to Sentence Embeddings}
\sourced{While Word2Vec produces vectors for individual words, RAG applications need \textbf{whole-sentence (or paragraph) embeddings}. Sentence-BERT \cite{reimers2019sbert}, built on the BERT architecture with a \textit{pooling} layer, produces dense vectors for entire sentences, optimized for semantic-similarity computation. The E5 family \cite{wang2022e5} pushes this idea further with weakly-supervised contrastive pre-training.}

\subsection{The multilingual-e5-base Model}
\humantext{Our first instinct was a small English-centric model, but it could not handle Arabic or Darija, which is half of our traffic. We therefore chose \textbf{intfloat/multilingual-e5-base} \cite{wang2024me5}: a single model that embeds French, Arabic, English, and Darija in the \emph{same} vector space. This is what lets us drop any translation step --- a Darija question and an Arabic page land close together directly. It produces 768-dimension vectors and has two practical rules we must respect:}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Prefixes:} documents are embedded with the prefix \texttt{passage:} and queries with \texttt{query:}. The model was trained this way, and skipping the prefixes noticeably hurts retrieval quality.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Normalization:} the vectors are L2-normalized, so an inner-product search equals cosine similarity (used in Section~\ref{sec:faiss}).}
\end{itemize}
\humantext{One multilingual model covering all four languages, with no translation layer to maintain, was worth more to us than the slightly smaller footprint of a monolingual model.}

\subsection{Cosine Similarity}
\humantext{Semantic search relies on the \textbf{cosine similarity} between two vectors:}
\begin{equation}
    \text{sim}(\vec{a}, \vec{b}) = \frac{\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}}{||\vec{a}|| \times ||\vec{b}||}
\end{equation}
\humantext{A score of 1 means a perfect match, while a score of 0 means no semantic relation at all. This metric is preferred because it is insensitive to vector length, measuring only the angle between the vectors. Because our e5 vectors are already normalized, this cosine is exactly the inner product the FAISS index computes.}

\section{Vector Databases: FAISS}
\label{sec:faiss}

\subsection{The Scaling Problem}
\humantext{Computing the cosine similarity between the query vector and every vector in the database exhaustively (\textit{brute force}) is fine for a few thousand documents, but becomes prohibitive for millions of vectors. Vector databases solve this with optimized index structures.}

\subsection{FAISS (Facebook AI Similarity Search)}
\sourced{FAISS, developed by Meta's research team (formerly Facebook) \cite{johnson2019faiss}, is an open-source library optimized for similarity search in high-dimensional vector spaces. FAISS offers several index types:}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{IndexFlatL2 / IndexFlatIP:} exhaustive (brute-force) search --- exact but slow on very large datasets. This is the index we use: on L2-normalized vectors, \texttt{IndexFlatIP} (inner product) computes exact cosine similarity, and our corpus (about \numChunks{} chunks) is small enough that brute-force search stays instant, so an approximate index is unnecessary.}
    \item \aitext{\textbf{IndexIVFFlat:} uses Voronoi partitioning to split the space into $n$ cells, sharply reducing the number of comparisons.}
    \item \aitext{\textbf{IndexIVFPQ:} combines IVF with Product Quantization, compressing the vectors to reduce the memory footprint.}
\end{itemize}

\humantext{In DjezzyBot, FAISS is orchestrated by the \textbf{LangChain} framework \cite{chase2022langchain}, which simplifies the chain of operations: loading documents $\rightarrow$ chunking $\rightarrow$ embedding $\rightarrow$ indexing $\rightarrow$ search $\rightarrow$ injection into the LLM prompt.}

\section{Model Quantization}

\subsection{Motivations}
\humantext{A 7-billion-parameter model stored in 16-bit floating point (FP16) takes about 14~GB of GPU memory. On Google Colab's free GPU (NVIDIA T4 with 15~GB of VRAM), that would leave almost no room for the model, the data, and the intermediate computations --- let alone the speech models. Quantization is what makes this model fit.}

\subsection{4-bit Quantization (NF4)}
\sourced{Quantization reduces the precision of a model's weights to shrink its memory footprint; the foundational work on 8-bit matrix multiplication for Transformers is by Dettmers et al. \cite{dettmers2022int8}. The \textbf{NF4} (\textit{NormalFloat 4-bit}) technique, introduced by Dettmers et al. \cite{dettmers2023qlora}, is specifically designed for the weight distributions of neural networks. It reduces each parameter from 16 or 32 bits down to 4 bits, with minimal loss of quality.}

\humantext{In this project, we use the following BitsAndBytes configuration:}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \humantext{\textbf{load\_in\_4bit = True}: loads the model in 4-bit precision.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{bnb\_4bit\_quant\_type = ``nf4''}: uses the optimal NormalFloat distribution.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{bnb\_4bit\_use\_double\_quant = True}: applies double quantization to further reduce the memory of the quantization constants.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{bnb\_4bit\_compute\_dtype = float16}: runs the computations in half precision for speed.}
\end{itemize}

\humantext{With this configuration, the 4-bit Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct model takes only about \textbf{5~GB of VRAM}. Together with the embedding model and Whisper, the resident footprint is around \vramResident~GB; the speech-synthesis model is loaded on demand, pushing the peak to about \vramPeak~GB --- comfortably within the T4's 15~GB.}

\section{Prompt Engineering}

\subsection{Definition}
\humantext{\textit{Prompt engineering} is the craft of phrasing the instructions given to the LLM to control its behavior precisely. In a RAG setting, the prompt is crucial: it is what forces the model to stick to the facts in the context and not hallucinate.}

\subsection{The ChatML Format}
\humantext{Qwen 2.5 uses the \textbf{ChatML} prompt format, which structures the conversation into distinct roles:}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \humantext{\texttt{<|im\_start|>system}: defines the assistant's persona and rules.}
    \item \humantext{\texttt{<|im\_start|>user}: holds the user's question and the RAG context.}
    \item \humantext{\texttt{<|im\_start|>assistant}: marks the start of the generated answer.}
\end{itemize}

\humantext{In DjezzyBot, the system prompt contains strict rules (``make no inference'', ``use only the context'', ``if the customer gives a budget, propose only offers at or below that price'') that act as guardrails against hallucination. The full set of rules is detailed in Chapter~3.}

\subsection{Principles}
\humantext{\textit{Web scraping} is the technique of automatically extracting data from websites. To build DjezzyBot's knowledge base, we need to collect all the information on offers, services, and prices published on Djezzy's official site.}

\subsection{Tools Used}

\section{Speech Processing: STT and TTS}
\humantext{To offer a multimodal interface (text and voice), a modern virtual assistant needs two key components.}

\subsection{Speech-to-Text (Speech Recognition)}
\sourced{The STT module transcribes audio to text. The current state of the art is dominated by \textbf{Whisper} (OpenAI) \cite{radford2023whisper}, an encoder-decoder Transformer trained on more than 680{,}000 hours of multilingual audio. Whisper is especially good at:}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \humantext{Handling \textbf{noisy environments}.}
    \item \humantext{Recognizing \textbf{code-switching} (alternating French and Arabic/Darija within one sentence).}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Automatic detection} of the spoken language.}
\end{itemize}

\humantext{We use \texttt{whisper-medium} rather than the smaller \texttt{whisper-small}: medium transcribes Arabic and Darija noticeably better, which matters for our users. To keep it affordable on the T4, we run it through \textbf{faster-whisper} (a CTranslate2 re-implementation) in \texttt{float16}, which holds the weights at about 1.5~GB instead of the roughly 3~GB of the original fp32 model, and is faster too.}

\subsection{Text-to-Speech (Speech Synthesis)}
\humantext{The TTS module turns the LLM's text answer into natural speech. We considered two families of solutions:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Cloud TTS (e.g. Microsoft Edge-TTS):} very natural neural voices with no VRAM cost on the inference machine. We tried this early, but it sends every answer to an external server --- which clashes with our goal of a fully local, sovereign, open-source stack --- so we did not keep it.}
    \item \sourced{\textbf{On-device neural TTS (VITS \cite{kim2021vits}, Coqui XTTS-v2 \cite{casanova2024xtts}):} models that run locally on the GPU. VITS is an end-to-end conditional VAE with adversarial training; XTTS-v2 extends this idea to a massively multilingual, zero-shot voice that covers French, English, and Arabic.}
\end{itemize}

\humantext{For this project we chose \textbf{Coqui XTTS-v2}: it is multilingual, it runs locally (no external API), and it is loaded \emph{on demand} --- only when a voice answer is actually requested --- so it costs no VRAM while the user is only typing. Darija has no dedicated XTTS voice, so we speak it with the Arabic voice, which is consistent with answering Darija in Modern Standard Arabic.}

\section{Linguistic Challenges: Darija and Code-Switching}
\humantext{Designing an assistant for an Algerian audience raises specific challenges that deserve particular attention.}

\subsection{Algerian Darija}
\humantext{Darija (Algerian dialectal Arabic) is the mother tongue of most of the Algerian population. Unlike Modern Standard Arabic (MSA), Darija:}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \humantext{Has \textbf{no standardized spelling}: the same word can be written in many ways.}
    \item \humantext{Is often written in \textbf{``Arabizi''} (Latin letters with digits) on social media: ``3'' for \textit{ain}, ``7'' for \textit{ha}, ``9'' for \textit{qaf}. Example: ``3tini l'forfait'' (give me the plan).}
    \item \humantext{Borrows heavily from \textbf{French}: ``forfait'', ``internet'', ``message''.}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Code-Switching}
\humantext{Algerian \textit{code-switching} is a phenomenon where a speaker alternates between two or more languages within one conversation, even within one sentence.} \humantext{For example, ``\textit{Bghit forfait fiha bezaf internet}'' (I want a plan with a lot of internet) mixes Darija (``bghit'', ``fiha'', ``bezaf'') with French (``forfait'', ``internet'').}

\subsection{Adaptation Strategies}
\humantext{Classic NLP models trained on Modern Standard Arabic struggle with this dialectal richness. Several strategies exist:}
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Fine-tuning:} adapt an existing LLM with an annotated Darija corpus. Costly and requires quality data.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{On-the-fly translation:} translate Darija into French before the LLM processes it. Simple in principle, but a translation step adds latency and can corrupt brand names and prices.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Robust multilingual models:} use a multilingual embedding model and LLM (such as Qwen) whose massive multilingual training lets them ``read'' dialectal sentences directly. \textbf{This is the strategy we adopted}: we embed Darija directly with \texttt{multilingual-e5} and let Qwen answer in MSA, with \emph{no} translation layer at all (see Chapter~3).}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Virtual Assistants in Telecom: State of Play}
\humantext{Virtual assistants in telecom are not new. However, most deployed solutions rely on proprietary architectures:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \aitext{\textbf{TOBi (Vodafone):} a proprietary chatbot built on commercial NLU engines, handling more than 70\,\% of customer requests without human intervention.}
    \item \aitext{\textbf{Djingo (Orange France):} an assistant using Nuance Communications' NLU for call routing and FAQs.}
    \item \aitext{\textbf{Google Duplex / Contact Center AI:} Google's cloud solution for automating phone call centers.}
\end{itemize}

\humantext{These solutions perform well, but they have major limitations for the Algerian context: no Darija support, high licensing costs, and dependence on foreign cloud infrastructure. DjezzyBot positions itself as an \textbf{open-source, sovereign, multilingual alternative}, fitted to Algeria's linguistic and economic realities.}

\section{Conclusion}
\humantext{This chapter laid out the theoretical foundations of modern generative AI and speech-processing technologies. The evolution of NLP, from rule-based systems to Transformers and LLMs, opened unprecedented possibilities for building intelligent virtual assistants. The RAG architecture, paired with vector databases such as FAISS and quantization techniques such as NF4, now makes it possible to deploy capable systems even on limited hardware.}

\humantext{Combining open-source models (Qwen 2.5, multilingual-e5, Whisper, Coqui XTTS-v2), the RAG architecture, and robust STT/TTS systems gives us the building blocks for a sovereign, accurate assistant fitted to the Algerian sociolinguistic context. The next chapter details the methodology we used to assemble these technologies into the DjezzyBot system.}

\subsection{Request Processing Flow}
\humantext{When a user submits a question (text or voice), the system runs the following flow:}

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Reception:} if the request is voice, Whisper-medium transcribes it to text.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Language detection:} the system identifies the language (French, Arabic, English, or Algerian Darija). There is no translation --- the question is later embedded directly in its own language.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Intent routing:} a rule-based classifier reads the question and selects one of \numRoutes{} routes \emph{before} any vector search. A competitor question short-circuits here to a canned, polite refusal.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Query expansion:} for the routes that hit the vector store, the question is enriched with Djezzy synonyms, and the offers, roaming, and budget cues it mentions are detected.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Retrieval:} dense FAISS search is run with the strategy chosen by the route; an off-domain question yields an empty result handled as a refusal.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Generation:} Qwen2.5-7B writes an answer based only on the retrieved context, following the system prompt's \numRules{} rules, with greedy (deterministic) decoding.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Output:} the answer is returned in the user's language, and spoken aloud (XTTS-v2) when the request came by voice.}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Data Extraction (Web Scraping)}

\subsection{Acquisition Strategy}
\humantext{Building the knowledge base relies on automatically extracting data from Djezzy's official sites (\url{https://www.djezzy.dz} and \url{https://www.djezzy5g.dz}). The scraper is \emph{self-discovering}: it finds the offer pages by itself, with no hand-written URL list, by combining three discovery layers:}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Sitemaps:} the scraper parses each domain's \texttt{/sitemap.xml} and \texttt{/sitemap\_index.xml} (handling sitemap-index files one level deep) for page URLs.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Breadth-first crawl (BFS):} starting from the sitemap seeds, it renders each page with Playwright and harvests every internal link, down to a maximum depth. This is how deep pages such as \texttt{/entreprises/offres/djezzy-legend-pro/} are found even though listing pages do not link to them cleanly.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Path hints:} a few known path patterns (e.g. \texttt{/particuliers/offres/}, \texttt{/roaming/}, \texttt{/5g/}) are probed on each domain as extra crawl seeds, so the offer and roaming sections are reached even if the homepage does not link to them.}
\end{enumerate}

\humantext{The crawl's real stopping condition is \textbf{frontier exhaustion}: it ends when there are no new internal links left to follow, which self-sizes to the site. Two limits --- a 1{,}500-page cap and a 45-minute wall-clock budget --- are only safety fuses against a runaway crawl, not targets.}

\subsection{Scraper Architecture}
\humantext{The scraper (\texttt{scraper.py}) is built around a few main functions.}

\subsubsection{Navigation with Playwright}
\humantext{Playwright is used as an automated browser to behave like a real user. This gives two major advantages:}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \humantext{\textbf{JavaScript execution:} unlike a plain HTTP request, Playwright runs the page's JavaScript, which is essential for modern sites that load their content dynamically. Some Djezzy offer pages (for example iZZY) never fire the \texttt{load} or \texttt{networkidle} events, so instead of waiting on those events we wait until the page's visible text grows past a threshold --- a sign that the real content has been injected.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Anti-bot avoidance:} the browser runs headless (\texttt{headless=True}) with a custom User-Agent imitating Chrome on Windows~10, which lowers the risk of being blocked by the site.}
\end{itemize}

\humantext{A \textbf{retry-with-delay} mechanism is implemented: if a page fails to load (network error, timeout), the system retries up to three times with a 2-second delay between attempts.}

\subsubsection{HTML Cleaning}
\humantext{One key part of this project is the HTML-cleaning function (\texttt{clean\_html}), the first defense against polluting the knowledge base. Without it, navigation menus, sidebars, and footers would end up injected into \textit{every} document, polluting the vector-search results.}

\humantext{The function works in two passes:}
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Removal by HTML tag:} dropping \texttt{<script>}, \texttt{<style>}, \texttt{<header>}, \texttt{<footer>}, \texttt{<nav>}, \texttt{<aside>}, \texttt{<noscript>}, \texttt{<iframe>}, and \texttt{<form>} entirely.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Removal by CSS attribute:} dropping any element whose \texttt{class} or \texttt{id} contains a sub-token typical of non-informative chrome: \texttt{menu}, \texttt{nav}, \texttt{footer}, \texttt{sidebar}, \texttt{breadcrumb}, \texttt{cookie}, \texttt{popup}, \texttt{modal}, \texttt{overlay}, \texttt{social}, \texttt{sticky}, \texttt{banner}, \texttt{mega}. Matching is done on sub-tokens (splitting on \texttt{-} and \texttt{\_}), and \texttt{<html>}/\texttt{<body>} are never removed --- a lesson learned when a naive substring match on ``modal-open'' on \texttt{<body>} once wiped a whole page.}
\end{enumerate}

\humantext{This two-pass approach keeps only the relevant editorial content (offer descriptions, price tables, activation conditions).}

\subsubsection{JSON Structuring}
\humantext{The cleaned text is stored as a normalized JSON record, where each entry holds four fields:}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \humantext{\texttt{title}: the page title (\texttt{<title>} tag, with an \texttt{<h1>} fallback).}
    \item \humantext{\texttt{url}: the source URL, so the information stays traceable.}
    \item \humantext{\texttt{content}: the full cleaned text, with blank lines collapsed.}
    \item \humantext{\texttt{scraped\_at}: the UTC timestamp of the crawl.}
\end{itemize}

\humantext{Only pages whose cleaned content exceeds 300 characters are kept, which drops empty pages and redirects.}

\section{Building the Vector Knowledge Base}

\subsection{Indexing Pipeline}
\humantext{Turning the raw JSON into a searchable vector database follows a four-step pipeline, orchestrated by the \textbf{LangChain} framework \cite{chase2022langchain}.}

\subsubsection{Step 1: Loading the Documents}
\humantext{The JSON file is loaded and each entry is converted into a LangChain \texttt{Document}, holding the raw text and its metadata (source URL, page title).}

\subsubsection{Step 2: Chunking}
\humantext{The documents are split with the \texttt{RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter}, \emph{page by page} so a chunk can never mix content from two different URLs, using these parameters:}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \humantext{\textbf{chunk\_size = 1{,}200 characters}: large enough to keep an offer's context (price, volume, validity) together, but small enough that a chunk stays focused on one topic.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{chunk\_overlap = 120 characters}: a 10\,\% overlap between adjacent chunks so information is not lost at the split boundaries.}
\end{itemize}

\humantext{The \texttt{RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter} is preferred over the plain \texttt{CharacterTextSplitter} because it tries to split on natural separators first (\texttt{\textbackslash n\textbackslash n}, \texttt{\textbackslash n}, \texttt{. }, \texttt{ }) before falling back to an arbitrary cut.}

\subsubsection{Step 3: Embedding}
\humantext{Each chunk is turned into a dense 768-dimension vector by the \textbf{multilingual-e5-base} model (see Chapter~2). Documents are embedded with the \texttt{passage:} prefix and the vectors are L2-normalized. This is done once (offline) and the result is saved to disk.}

\subsubsection{Step 4: FAISS Indexing}
\humantext{The vectors are inserted into a FAISS \texttt{IndexFlatIP} (inner product) index, which performs an exhaustive dot-product search; on normalized vectors this is exactly cosine similarity. The corpus is modest --- \numPages{} pages give about \numChunks{} chunks --- so exhaustive search stays well under a few milliseconds, and no approximate index is needed.}

\humantext{The FAISS index and its associated \textit{docstore} are saved locally (and, on Colab, mirrored to Google Drive), so the embeddings are not recomputed at every restart of the notebook.}

\section{The Intent Router}

\subsection{Problem}
\humantext{Our early tests showed that dense vector search alone (cosine similarity via FAISS) had several weaknesses:}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Missed offer names:} the embedding model did not always rank an offer's own page first for a coined commercial name (``Campuce'', ``iZZY''), because these words appear in no dictionary.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Comparison imbalance:} for ``Legend vs iZZY'', dense search tended to return many chunks of one offer and only a few of the other, biasing the comparison.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Vague catalogue questions:} very general questions (``what offers do you have?'') produced an uneven mix instead of one line per family.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Budget and roaming confusion:} a budget such as ``500 DA'' has to be filtered numerically, and a foreign roaming tariff must never be answered with a national price.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Competitors:} a question about Ooredoo or Mobilis must be refused, but a Djezzy offer that mentions ``calls to Mobilis'' must still be answered.}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Solution: a Rule-Based Router with Seven Routes}
\humantext{To solve these problems, we built a rule-based intent router (\texttt{smart\_retrieve}). It reads the user's question \emph{before} touching FAISS and chooses one of \numRoutes{} routes. Crucially, this is \emph{not} a hybrid retriever (there is no BM25 and no neural re-ranker): the vector search is pure dense FAISS, and the router decides \emph{how} to query it. The routes are tried in a fixed priority order, so an ambiguous question is handled by the most specific rule that matches:}

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Competitor} --- the question names Ooredoo or Mobilis (Latin or Arabic script) as its subject. The router returns a sentinel and the bot answers with a polite ``Djezzy only'' refusal, with no LLM call. A brand mentioned only as a \emph{call destination} (``calls to Mobilis'') is allowed through.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Roaming} --- a destination, or Hadj/Umrah (including the Arabic forms), is mentioned. Roaming chunks are preferred. This route wins over budget, so a foreign tariff is never filtered out as if it were a national price.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Catalogue} --- a ``list your offers'' question with no specific offer named. The router returns one priced snippet per family, \textbf{sorted cheapest-first in Python}.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Comparison} --- a comparison cue plus at least two named offers. Each offer's own page is pulled (so both are guaranteed present), and the offers are ordered cheapest-first.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Budget} --- an amount plus a budget cue (``I have 500~DA''). The router pulls a pool of priced chunks and \textbf{filters the prices in Python} ($\le$ budget); the LLM never does the filtering. This is the weakest route, because Djezzy pages mix subscription prices with per-unit tariffs.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Named offer} --- a single offer is named. The router returns that offer's \emph{whole} page (all tiers), with the tiers ordered cheapest-first in Python --- the fix for Cam Puce, whose seven tiers used to come back in arbitrary order --- plus a couple of dense chunks for context.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Normal} --- the fallback: dense search with $k=5$, guarded against out-of-domain questions (Section~\ref{sec:ood}).}
\end{enumerate}

\humantext{The named offer presence is the answer to the ``Campuce'' problem from Chapter~1: rather than hoping the embedding model understands an invented name, the router walks the FAISS docstore and selects the chunks whose text literally contains the offer name (whole-word, accent-insensitive), guaranteeing the right offer is present even when dense ranking would have missed it.}

\subsection{Deterministic Cheapest-First Ordering}
\humantext{Every route that presents priced offers orders them \textbf{cheapest-first in Python}, never relying on the LLM to sort. One shared helper splits an offer's text into tier blocks at each subscription-price boundary (carefully skipping per-unit rates like ``5~DA/SMS'' and crédit/bonus amounts, which are not tier prices) and reorders them ascending. Because the context handed to the model is \emph{already} in the right order, the answer's order no longer depends on the model obeying an instruction like ``list from cheapest to most expensive''. This single principle removed both the ``random tier order'' bug and the ``dumps everything'' bug at the source.}

\subsection{Context Assembly}
\humantext{We deliberately kept retrieval simple: there is \emph{no} second-stage neural re-ranker (such as a cross-encoder) and \emph{no} lexical BM25 stage. The router already selects the right chunks and orders the priced ones cheapest-first, so a re-ranker would add latency and a model to load for little gain on a corpus this small.}

\humantext{The selected chunks are concatenated into a single context block, each prefixed with a \texttt{[Source: url]} header for traceability, and the block is capped at \texttt{MAX\_CONTEXT\_CHARS = 8000} characters. This cap is large enough to hold a full named-offer page or one snippet per family in a catalogue listing, while keeping the prompt short enough for fast generation on the T4. Because the chunks arrive already ordered, truncation drops only the least relevant tail.}

\section{Lexicon: Query Expansion and the Competitor Firewall}

\subsection{Query Expansion}
\humantext{The lexicon (\texttt{data/lexicon.py}) holds a small, hand-built vocabulary used \emph{before} the question is embedded. \texttt{expand\_synonyms} appends Djezzy-specific terms for any phrase it recognizes, so the embedding sees the right vocabulary even when the user wrote slang or a generic word.}

\humantext{\textbf{Example:} when the user types ``\textit{je veux beaucoup de gigas}'', the lexicon detects ``gigas'' and ``beaucoup'' and appends ``go data volume'' and ``max grand volume go'', giving the embedder a clearer signal.}

\humantext{A few categories the lexicon covers:}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Technical synonyms:} ``gigas'' / ``data'' $\rightarrow$ ``go internet volume''.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Price intent:} ``prix'' / ``combien'' $\rightarrow$ ``tarif da forfait''.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Audience:} ``étudiant'' / ``jeunes'' $\rightarrow$ ``campuce cam puce student''.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Roaming:} ``étranger'' / ``voyage'' $\rightarrow$ ``roaming international''.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Darija verbs:} ``bghit'' / ``nhab'' $\rightarrow$ ``je veux offre forfait''.}
\end{itemize}

\humantext{The same lexicon also provides the offer names, roaming destinations, budget cues, comparison cues, and Darija markers that the router uses to detect intent and the language detector uses to spot Darija.}

\subsection{The Competitor Firewall}
\humantext{The router includes a safety mechanism for mentions of competing operators (Ooredoo, Mobilis, and their Arabic spellings). When a competitor is the \emph{subject} of the question, the router returns a sentinel that short-circuits the whole pipeline, and the bot replies with a canned, polite refusal in the user's language, for example:}

\begin{quote}
    \textit{``I am Djezzy's virtual assistant and cannot provide information about other operators. I'd be glad to tell you about Djezzy's offers.''}
\end{quote}

\humantext{One subtlety is worth noting: a bare brand mention is not always a competitor question. A Djezzy offer legitimately advertises ``unlimited calls to Ooredoo and Mobilis'', so the router refuses only when no call-destination cue (``vers'', ``calls to''\ldots) precedes the brand. This avoids wrongly refusing a real Djezzy question. Because it runs in pure Python (no LLM), the refusal is instant and fully reliable.}

\section{Multilingual Handling Without Translation}

\subsection{No Translation Layer}
\humantext{An early design translated every non-French question into French, queried the base, and translated the answer back. We dropped this entirely. Translation added latency, and the Opus-MT models we tried corrupted brand names and prices --- ``\textit{عروض جيزي ليجند}'' (Djezzy Legend offers) once came back as ``offres de Jezzie pour le recrutement'', with the model reading ``\textit{ليجند}'' (Legend) as the Arabic word for ``recruitment''. Working around this with placeholder tokens was fragile and never fully reliable.}

\humantext{The multilingual embedding model removes the need for all of this. Because \texttt{multilingual-e5} places French, Arabic, English, and Darija in the same vector space, a question is embedded \emph{directly} in its own language and still matches the right (often Arabic or French) page. For the answer, we rely on Qwen2.5's native multilingual ability: a short directive placed in the \emph{user} turn (``Reply entirely in English'', ``\textit{أجب بالكامل باللغة العربية الفصحى}'') tells the model which language to answer in, with no separate translation step and nothing to corrupt the prices.}

\subsection{Language Detection}
\humantext{Detection runs in three priority layers, because \texttt{langdetect} systematically mistakes Darija for French (they share French loanwords):}
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Darija markers} (highest priority): if any token belongs to a hand-built Darija word list (``bghit'', ``bezaf'', ``chhal''\ldots), the language is set to Darija (\texttt{dz}).}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Arabic script}: any character in the Arabic Unicode range marks the question as Arabic (\texttt{ar}).}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Statistical detection}: \texttt{langdetect} distinguishes French from English, defaulting to French on failure.}
\end{enumerate}
\humantext{A Darija question is detected as \texttt{dz} but answered in Modern Standard Arabic, following the directive above --- which is why no Darija ``translation'' step is needed at all.}

\section{Out-of-Domain Handling}
\label{sec:ood}

\humantext{DjezzyBot must refuse questions outside Djezzy's domain (weather, jokes, politics) without wrongly refusing a real customer. We handle this in \textbf{two layers}, and the design is backed by a calibration experiment that we consider one of the project's genuine findings.}

\subsection{Why a Score Threshold Does Not Work}
\humantext{The obvious approach is to refuse when the best retrieved chunk scores below a similarity threshold. We calibrated such a threshold on the real index, using about 95 queries in all four languages, and found that it simply cannot separate on-topic from off-topic questions. With \texttt{multilingual-e5}, \emph{everything} scores high and the bands overlap (Table~\ref{tab:ood_calib}): real customer questions, off-topic noise, and even keyboard-mash gibberish all land in roughly the same 0.77--0.85 range --- and gibberish sometimes scores \emph{higher} than a short real question. The lowest real score we observed was a short Darija question at 0.774.}

\subsection{The Two-Layer Design}
\humantext{Since the score cannot judge relevance, we do not let it. Instead:}
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \humantext{\textbf{A low score floor} (\texttt{OOD\_MIN\_SIMILARITY = 0.70}) trips only on degenerate input (a broken embedding, a best match below everything observed). It is set far below the real-customer band, so it never fires on genuine traffic.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{The LLM's domain-scope rule} (rule~1 of the system prompt) is the real judge: it decides by \emph{meaning} whether a question is about Djezzy, and politely declines anything that is not.}
\end{enumerate}
\humantext{This is a deliberate trade-off. A cleverly phrased off-topic question could occasionally slip a partial answer through, but in exchange no real customer --- especially a short Darija question that scores low --- is ever refused on a borderline score. For a customer-facing assistant, we judge that trade worthwhile.}

\section{Applied Prompt Engineering}

\subsection{Structure of the System Prompt}
\humantext{The system prompt is the most critical piece of the RAG pipeline: it defines the assistant's identity, its constraints, and its behavior. DjezzyBot's system prompt is built around \numRules{} strict rules:}

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Djezzy domain only:} answer only questions about Djezzy (offers, prices, services, recharge, roaming, network); politely decline anything else in one sentence, even if the answer is known. This rule is the off-domain judge of Section~\ref{sec:ood}.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Context only:} never invent a price, offer, volume, validity, or USSD code; if the information is not in the context, say so honestly.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Exact price, level of detail:} each cited offer carries its exact price; follow the presentation directive (brief or full) supplied with the question, and when full detail is asked, omit nothing present in the context.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Budget:} if a budget is given, show only offers at or below it; the context is already filtered, so list everything in it.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{National $\ne$ roaming:} never confuse a national tariff with a roaming (abroad) tariff.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{No competitor (with one exception):} never describe or recommend another operator's offers, but a Djezzy offer \emph{may} mention calls/SMS \emph{toward} other networks.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Customer's language:} always answer in the language of the question.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Darija $\rightarrow$ MSA:} if the question is in Algerian Darija, answer in clear Modern Standard Arabic.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Latin names:} keep offer and destination names in their original Latin script (Legend, iZZY, Campuce\ldots), even in an Arabic answer.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{No repetition:} do not repeat the same offer or sentence twice.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Concise:} answer clearly, in a structured and concise way.}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Why These Rules}
\humantext{Each rule was added in response to a concrete problem seen in testing:}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Rule~1} keeps the bot inside its domain, refusing off-topic questions by meaning rather than by keyword.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Rule~2} stops the model from inventing non-existent plans.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Rule~4} was added after a ``1{,}000 DA'' test produced recommendations at 7{,}500 DA; it also clarifies that a crédit/bonus amount inside an offer may exceed the budget without being the price.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Rule~5} fixed the national-vs-roaming confusion, where a foreign tariff was answered with a national price.}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Greedy Decoding (do\_sample=False)}
\humantext{The generation parameter \texttt{do\_sample=False} forces \textbf{greedy} decoding: at each step the model picks the highest-probability token, with no randomness. This is crucial in a factual RAG setting, where any ``creativity'' turns into hallucination (mixing prices between offers, inventing plans). It also makes the answers reproducible, which is what lets our acceptance tests assert exact behavior.}

\section{Voice Module Architecture}

\subsection{STT Module (Speech-to-Text)}
\humantext{The speech-recognition module uses \textbf{Whisper-medium}, run through \texttt{faster-whisper} (a CTranslate2 re-implementation) in \texttt{float16}. The processing flow is:}
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \humantext{The user records a voice message through Gradio's microphone component.}
    \item \humantext{The audio file is passed to Whisper, which auto-detects the language.}
    \item \humantext{If Whisper guesses a language we do not support, it re-transcribes while forcing the most probable supported language, so the transcription text itself is corrected, not just its label.}
    \item \humantext{The transcribed text is fed into the same RAG pipeline used by the text path.}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{TTS Module (Text-to-Speech)}
\humantext{The speech-synthesis module uses \textbf{Coqui XTTS-v2}, loaded \emph{on demand} the first time a voice answer is requested (so it costs no VRAM while the user only types). The voice is chosen from the detected language:}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \humantext{French, English, and Arabic use their respective XTTS-v2 voices.}
    \item \humantext{Darija has no dedicated XTTS voice, so it is spoken with the Arabic voice --- consistent with answering Darija in Modern Standard Arabic.}
\end{itemize}
\humantext{Before synthesis, the text is normalized for natural pronunciation: thousands separators are removed, telecom units (Go, Mo, SMS, DA) are expanded to spoken words in the right language, and USSD/short codes and special numbers are read digit by digit.}

\section{Introduction}

\section{Deployment Environment and Technology Transition}

\subsection{Limits of Cloud APIs and Why We Went Local}
\humantext{At the start of the project, a first version of DjezzyBot used Groq's cloud API for LLM inference (Llama 3). It was very fast, but it had two major drawbacks:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Quota limits and instability:} the strict quotas on the free Groq API caused rate-limit errors during repeated testing, blocking user sessions.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Data sovereignty and privacy:} sending Algerian subscribers' queries to third-party servers abroad raises compliance concerns for a long-standing telecom operator like Djezzy.}
\end{itemize}
\humantext{For these reasons, we moved to a 100\,\% local, open-source inference stack. We chose \textbf{Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct} from Alibaba Cloud for its multilingual instruction-following and its strong native Arabic support.}

\subsection{Hardware Setup (Google Colab T4)}
\humantext{To work around the lack of a dedicated GPU on local workstations, the inference infrastructure is deployed on \textbf{Google Colab}, on an \textbf{NVIDIA T4} GPU with 15~GB of VRAM. To persist the data and the knowledge index, the environment syncs with Google Drive by mounting the project folder. This avoids recomputing the FAISS index and reloading the JSON knowledge base (\texttt{data/djezzy\_pages.json}) at every Colab session restart.}

\section{Implementation of the Core Components}

\subsection{Loading the LLM and 4-bit Quantization}
\humantext{Loading Qwen2.5-7B in native precision (FP16) takes about 14~GB of VRAM, which would saturate the T4 once Whisper and the embedding model are added. We therefore use \textbf{NF4} (\textit{Normal Float 4}) quantization via the \texttt{bitsandbytes} library and \texttt{unsloth}'s pre-quantized weights, which compresses the model to about \textbf{5~GB} of VRAM.}

\humantext{The model- and tokenizer-loading code is shown in Listing~\ref{lst:load_model}.}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={Loading and 4-bit quantization of Qwen2.5}, label={lst:load_model}]
import torch
from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForCausalLM, BitsAndBytesConfig

model_id = "unsloth/Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct-bnb-4bit"

# NF4 quantization configuration
bnb_config = BitsAndBytesConfig(
    load_in_4bit=True,
    bnb_4bit_use_double_quant=True,
    bnb_4bit_quant_type="nf4",
    bnb_4bit_compute_dtype=torch.float16
)

# Load the tokenizer and the quantized model
tokenizer = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(model_id)
model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(
    model_id,
    quantization_config=bnb_config,
    device_map={"": 0}
)
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{The Intent Router (classify\_route)}
\humantext{The router is the single source of truth for the user's intent. A pure function, \texttt{classify\_route}, reads the question and returns one of the seven routes in strict priority order. Both retrieval and the answer style are driven by its result, so they can never drift apart --- the disconnect that once caused the ``dumps everything'' and ``random order'' bugs. Listing~\ref{lst:smart_retrieve} shows this dispatch logic.}

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={Priority-ordered intent classification}, label={lst:smart_retrieve}]
def classify_route(query):
    # Priority: competitor > roaming > catalogue > comparison
    #           > budget > named > normal
    if _is_competitor_query(query):
        return "competitor"
    offers = lexicon.detect_offers(query)
    if _roaming_markers(query):               # destination / Hadj / Umrah
        return "roaming"                      # wins over budget
    if _is_catalogue_query(query, offers) and not _is_budget_query(query):
        return "catalogue"
    if _is_comparison_query(query, offers):   # comparison cue + >= 2 offers
        return "comparison"
    if _is_budget_query(query):               # amount + budget cue
        return "budget"
    if offers:                                # a single offer named
        return "named"
    return "normal"
\end{lstlisting}

\humantext{Each priced route then orders offers cheapest-first \emph{in Python}. The named-offer route, for instance, returns the offer's whole page and sorts its tier blocks by ascending subscription price (skipping per-unit rates and crédit/bonus amounts), so the model never has to sort --- the fix for Cam Puce's seven tiers coming back in arbitrary order.}

\subsection{Query Expansion and the Competitor Firewall}
\humantext{Before a question is embedded, the lexicon expands it with Djezzy vocabulary, and the competitor firewall refuses questions whose \emph{subject} is a rival operator. Listing~\ref{lst:lexical} shows both, including the call-destination exception that keeps a real Djezzy question (``calls to Mobilis'') from being wrongly refused.}

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={Query expansion and competitor detection}, label={lst:lexical}]
def expand_synonyms(query):
    q = query.lower()
    extras = []
    for phrase, expansion in SYNONYMS.items():   # e.g. "gigas" -> "go data volume"
        if phrase in q:
            extras.append(expansion)
    return query + " " + " ".join(extras) if extras else query

def _is_competitor_query(query):
    q = _fold(query)
    if any(g in q for g in COMPETITOR_GENERIC):   # "concurrent", "competitor"
        return True
    for b in COMPETITOR_BRANDS:                    # Ooredoo, Mobilis, Arabic forms
        i = q.find(_fold(b))
        if i == -1:
            continue
        before = q[max(0, i - 30):i]
        # the brand is the SUBJECT unless a call cue ("vers", "calls to")
        # appears just before it -> then it is only a call destination
        if not any(cue in before for cue in CALL_DEST_CUES):
            return True
    return False
\end{lstlisting}

\section{User Interface (Gradio) and the Voice Round-Trip}

\subsection{Interface Structure}
\humantext{The interface is built with Gradio, themed in Djezzy's red and white. It is deliberately a \emph{single screen}: text and voice feed one shared conversation. The layout has one chat window (the shared history), a text box, a microphone, an audio player for the spoken reply, and clear/refresh buttons with a status bar. Typing answers in text; speaking transcribes the question into the same chat and also plays the answer aloud. The language is auto-detected from the input, so there is no manual language selector.}

\subsection{The Voice Round-Trip}
\humantext{The voice path reuses the exact same ``brain'' as the text path: the microphone audio is transcribed, the transcription goes through the identical retrieval, routing, and generation, and the answer is then synthesized. Because both paths call the same \texttt{generate\_answer}, voice and text can never drift apart. Each stage is timed separately for the latency measurements in Chapter~5. Listing~\ref{lst:voice} shows the round-trip.}

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={Voice round-trip (STT -> shared brain -> TTS)}, label={lst:voice}]
def voice_answer(audio_path, vector_db, history=None):
    stages = {}
    with timed(stages, "stt"):
        transcription, lang = transcribe(audio_path)   # Whisper-medium

    # Same retrieval/routing/generation as the text path
    res = generate_answer(transcription, lang, vector_db, history)
    stages["retrieval"]  = res["t_retrieval"]
    stages["generation"] = res["t_generation"]

    with timed(stages, "tts"):
        wav_path = synthesize(res["text"], lang)        # XTTS-v2, on demand

    record_latency("voice", res["route"], stages)
    return {"transcription": transcription, "text": res["text"],
            "lang": lang, "route": res["route"], "wav_path": wav_path}
\end{lstlisting}

By fitting the model within the VRAM budget through 4-bit quantization, by routing intent with a single deterministic classifier shared between retrieval and presentation, and by sharing one ``brain'' between the text and voice paths, we obtained a dependable, sovereign voice-and-text chatbot. The next chapter covers the acceptance tests and the quantitative evaluation of the system.}

\humantext{The single failing scenario is the budget route, and the cause is data quality, not routing. \chapter*{General Conclusion and Future Work}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{General Conclusion and Future Work}

\humantext{This thesis was devoted to the design and implementation of \textbf{DjezzyBot}, a multilingual virtual assistant (French, Modern Standard Arabic, English, and Algerian Darija) meant to improve customer service for the mobile operator Djezzy in Algeria. Faced with the recurring overload of call centers and the absence of NLP solutions fitted to the Algerian sociolinguistic landscape, we built a complete prototype based on the RAG (\textit{Retrieval-Augmented Generation}) architecture and open-source large language models, running entirely on a single free Google Colab T4 GPU.}

\humantext{Our key contributions are:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \humantext{A \textbf{rule-based intent router with \numRoutes{} routes} that reads each question before any vector search and orders priced offers cheapest-first \emph{in Python}, solving the failure modes of pure dense retrieval (missed coined names like \textit{Campuce} and \textit{iZZY}, comparison imbalance, budget filtering, roaming-vs-national confusion) without any hybrid BM25 or re-ranker.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Multilingual handling without a translation layer}: \texttt{multilingual-e5} embeds all four languages directly and Qwen2.5 answers in the user's language, removing the brittle Opus-MT pivot and its brand-name corruption entirely.}
    \item \humantext{A \textbf{two-layer out-of-domain design} backed by a calibration experiment showing that an embedding-score threshold cannot separate real questions from off-topic ones --- a genuine, defensible finding --- so relevance is judged by meaning, not by score.}
    \item \humantext{A \textbf{fully local, open-source voice-and-text stack} (Qwen2.5-7B 4-bit, multilingual-e5, Whisper-medium, Coqui XTTS-v2 on demand) that fits a 15~GB T4 and keeps all subscriber data on-premises.}
\end{itemize}

\humantext{On \numTestsTotal{} acceptance scenarios across four languages, DjezzyBot passes \textbf{\numTestsPass{}/\numTestsTotal{}} (about 92\,\%), with the single failure being the budget route, whose weakness we trace to the way Djezzy's pages mix subscription prices with per-unit tariffs.}

\humantext{This work opens several directions for future research and development:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Fine-tuning a dialectal embedder}: training an embedding model on large Algerian Darija corpora to push retrieval quality on dialectal and code-switched queries even further.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Information-system integration}: connecting the bot to Djezzy's back-office APIs for personalized, real-time requests (the subscriber's actual balance, activating options directly from the bot).}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Private-infrastructure deployment}: migrating the local quantized stack to Djezzy's own servers for operational sovereignty and scalability.}
    \item \humantext{\textbf{Strengthening the budget route}: a cleaner separation of subscription prices from per-unit tariffs in the data, to fix the one remaining failing scenario.}
\end{itemize}
